Afya Basics Chapter 1
Chapter 1 · The problem and the evidence

Care has moved home.
Shopping for it has not.

Chronic and family care in Tanzania is now carried out mainly by households, month after month. The retail system that supplies them was built for one-off, acute purchases: it is spread across more than eleven thousand small outlets, keeps no customer record, does not replenish and offers no reliable way to verify what is genuine. This chapter sets out the evidence for that gap and explains why it is a recurring and addressable retail market.

01 · DEMAND

Chronic disease is widespread in Tanzania, and control is the exception.

High blood pressure affects almost one Tanzanian adult in four, yet only about two in every hundred of those affected have it under control. The failure is not a lack of medicine or of medical knowledge. It is a failure of continuity, and continuity is a supply problem.

23.1%

of Tanzanian adults aged 18 to 69 had raised blood pressure in the 2023 national STEPS survey, rising to 46.4% among those aged 46 to 69: almost one in two.1

A condition managed at home, not in the clinic

Hypertension gives no warning for years. It is found only by measuring it, and it is kept under control only through a routine that never stops: a tablet every day, a reading every week or two, a clinic visit when the numbers change, and a refill before the last strip runs out. A doctor diagnoses the condition once; the household manages it on every other day of the year.

That routine has a physical supply chain behind it. It needs tablets in the right strength, a monitor that works, batteries for the monitor, a cuff that fits, and often test strips or a glucose meter for the diabetes that frequently comes with it. When any one of these is missing, the routine breaks, usually quietly, and the condition drifts out of control without symptoms.

Where patients are lost

The most recent national survey to follow people through every stage of care, from having the condition to having it controlled, shows how often that routine breaks. Of every hundred adults with hypertension, 17.6 knew they had it, 8.3 were being treated and 2.2 had their blood pressure under control.2 The chart shows the losses step by step. The largest by far comes first: 82 of every hundred are never diagnosed. After diagnosis, more than half do not start or stay on treatment, and of those who do, roughly three in four are still not controlled.

Diabetes shows the same structure. In the 2023 survey, 72.4% of people found to have diabetes had never been told they had it.3

Investment implication

The losses fall at two points a retailer can act on. The first is detection: a free blood-pressure check at the store entrance reaches people who would never book a clinic visit to find out. The second is continuity: a monthly box, delivered on a fixed date, removes the most common reason a treated patient stops being treated, which is simply running out. Both are central to the Afya Basics model rather than add-ons, and both turn a medical need into recurring, predictable monthly demand.

02 · SUPPLY

Supply is fragmented across more than eleven thousand small outlets.

Tanzanians already buy health products in shops; the category does not need to be created. What is missing is a retailer organised around the household and its monthly needs, rather than around one transaction at a time.

The shape of the market

Most medicines and health products reach Tanzanian households through Accredited Drug Dispensing Outlets (ADDOs): small, licensed shops permitted to sell a defined list of medicines. In 2018 the Pharmacy Council recorded about 11,356 ADDOs against 1,334 registered pharmacies, a ratio of 8.5 to 1.4 Set against the 2022 census population of 61.7 million, that is roughly one ADDO for every 5,400 people and one pharmacy for every 46,000.12 About one acutely ill person in three goes to an ADDO first, before any clinic.5

Built for the fever, not for the month

These outlets do one job very well. They are close to home, open early and late, and treat a fever, a cough or a stomach upset in minutes. That is exactly the need they were designed for: acute, unplanned and solved in one visit.

Chronic and family care is a different kind of need. It is planned, it repeats every month, and it involves many products at once. A household caring for a diabetic parent and a new baby needs tablets, strips, lancets, nappies in a size that changes every few months, wipes, formula and vitamins. A single small outlet cannot carry that range, keeps no record of what the family bought last month, cannot predict when it will run out, and does not deliver. The work of remembering, checking and searching therefore falls on the household, every month.

Why the gap has not closed

Each outlet is independent and small, so none has the scale to hold a wide range, invest in a customer record or run a delivery service. Pharmacies are fewer, concentrated in town centres and also organised around the single prescription. Neither format is built to own the household relationship, and that relationship is where the recurring value lies.

Investment implication

Afya Basics does not have to persuade anyone to start buying health products. Its task is to consolidate demand that is now split across thousands of small outlets into one relationship that knows the household: its products, sizes, brands and refill dates. Once that record exists, the next month’s order is a confirmation rather than a new search, and that is what makes a customer stay.

03 · TRUST

Authenticity cannot be verified at the point of sale.

A buyer cannot see whether a medicine or a test strip is genuine, in date and properly stored. Because the risk is invisible to the customer, trust has to be proven by the seller, and very few sellers are equipped to prove it.

1 in 10

medical products in low- and middle-income countries is substandard or falsified, according to the World Health Organization, which puts the share at an estimated 10.5%.6

How a bad product does harm

The damage is rarely dramatic; it is cumulative. A falsified test strip gives a wrong glucose reading, and a wrong reading leads to a wrong dose of insulin. A blood-pressure tablet stored in a hot shop loses strength, so the patient takes it faithfully and still is not controlled. A product sold past its expiry date may do nothing at all. In each case the patient believes they are being treated, which is worse than knowing they are not.

The risk is current

In September 2026 the Tanzania Medicines and Medical Devices Authority (TMDA) seized unregistered medicines worth TSh 360 million at Dar es Salaam’s international airport, among them treatments for diabetes and high blood pressure, the same conditions described in section 01.7

Take a household that buys twelve health products a month: tablets, strips, a syrup, vitamins and baby items. If each product independently had a one-in-ten chance of failing the standard, the chance that all twelve are sound in a given month would be:

A deliberately simple illustration: the WHO rate is an average across many countries and product types, and real risks are not independent. It shows why the risk feels constant to a family that buys many products every month, even when each single purchase is probably fine.

Investment implication

Proven provenance is both a reason to switch and a reason to stay. Afya Basics sells registered products only, buys from licensed distributors, records the batch number of every unit sold and blocks any expired or recalled batch at the till (chapter 9). If a recall is issued, it can contact every customer who bought the affected batch. A small outlet cannot offer any of this, and in a market where trust cannot be assumed, it is a lasting point of difference.

04 · CITY

Dar es Salaam has outgrown the way it shops.

The city has added close to three million people in twenty years. Its shops have multiplied to keep up, but the way they serve customers has barely changed, and congestion now makes every extra trip expensive.

Twenty years of growth

The Dar es Salaam region grew from about 2.49 million people in 2002 to 5.38 million in 2022, an increase of 116%.8 Growth was fastest in the first decade, about 5.8% a year, adding roughly 187,000 people a year; it slowed to about 2.1% a year in the second, still adding about 102,000 people a year, close to 280 new residents every day. The region now has more than 1.5 million households, and two thirds of its people are of working age.11

More shops, same format

Retail has grown in number, not in form. Advice and payment still happen at the same single counter, so a two-minute question about a dose waits behind every other customer. Ranges are narrow, so a list of four items often needs three shops. Delivery is rare. And congestion has grown with the population: a trip across town for a single item can take an hour in each direction, and much longer in the rain.

Who pays for the search

For a working household the cost is direct. Every evening spent driving between shops is time taken from work, rest or family, and it is repeated every month because nothing is remembered. The households most affected, salaried families caring for children or older parents, are also the ones most able to pay for a service that removes the search.

Investment implication

Density and congestion favour a model that brings the purchase to the household rather than the household to the purchase. One well-located flagship, combined with scheduled monthly delivery and same-day delivery for urgent items, can serve a far larger catchment than its floor area alone would suggest, and the denser the city becomes, the stronger that advantage grows.

05 · CHANNEL

Mobile payment and messaging are already the default.

The digital tools Afya Basics depends on, paying by phone, ordering by message and receiving reminders, are not new to its customers. They are how Tanzanians already pay and communicate, so the channel needs no customer education.

The scale of the rails

By March 2026 Tanzania had 80.98 million mobile-money accounts and 58.9 million internet subscriptions, with smartphone penetration at 42.5%.9 There are more mobile-money accounts than people, because many adults hold more than one. Paying a bill by phone, choosing a product from a picture and answering a WhatsApp reminder are everyday habits.

What the phone does for the model

For a membership business, the phone does three jobs that would otherwise cost money. It collects payment automatically each month, at a fraction of the cost and risk of cash. It carries the reminder that turns a one-off purchase into a habit: the message two days before the box is due, the alert when a child moves up a nappy size. And it lets a household change its order in seconds, which keeps members from leaving when their needs change.

The diaspora as a second market

Tanzanians abroad sent home TSh 3.3 trillion in 2024/25,10 about US$1.25 billion at an exchange rate of TSh 2,640. Much of that money is meant for the care of parents and relatives, yet the sender cannot see whether it became care. A box ordered and paid for from abroad, delivered to a parent’s door and confirmed with a photograph, turns a cash transfer into a visible, verifiable result.

Investment implication

Existing payment and messaging rails lower the cost of signing members up, billing them and keeping them. The diaspora adds a paying customer group outside Tanzania whose main requirement, proof that the money became care, is something the model provides by design.

06 · THE HOUSEHOLD

The cost to one household: almost five hours and an incomplete list.

Statistics describe the market; one household shows how the problem actually works. What follows is a single ordinary evening, told hour by hour.

Juma is an accountant in central Dar es Salaam; his wife Neema is at home with their seven-month-old son. Before he leaves for work she gives him a list of four things: nappies in size four, wipes, iron tablets and a fever medicine. Nothing on the list is rare or expensive. The family is illustrative, but every step of their evening is common in the city.

At two minutes past five Neema calls: the baby is a little warm. The fever medicine, one item among four in the morning, is now urgent. Juma leaves work at a quarter to six and spends more than two hours in traffic, slowed further by rain. The first shop has sold out of nappies. The second has nappies, but not in size four, and no wipes. The third has the fever medicine only as a loose bottle, with no box and no visible expiry date. He reaches home at twenty to eleven with two of the four items, one of them the wrong size.

Why the evening repeats

Nothing about this evening is remembered. Next month the same list will be written again, and the same search will begin, because no shop knows that this family needs size four now and size five in three months, or that the iron tablets run out every thirty days. Of the 4 hours 55 minutes, 3 hours 45 minutes were spent travelling and 1 hour 10 minutes in shops. If an evening like this happens once a month, it adds up to about 59 hours a year: more than seven working days spent looking for basic health products.

  • Urgent items by same-day delivery, ordered in thirty seconds
Investment implication

The household’s problem is not price; it is time, uncertainty and memory. A service that removes all three is worth paying for, and because the need returns every month, the relationship it creates is recurring by nature. What this evidence does not show

Limits we state openly

  • The blood-pressure care cascade comes from the 2012 survey, the latest that measured awareness, treatment and control together. The 2023 survey measured how common the condition is, not the full cascade.
  • The outlet counts are from 2018. The number of ADDOs has grown since; a newer count would strengthen, not weaken, the point about fragmentation.
  • The WHO estimate covers low- and middle-income countries as a group, not Tanzania alone.
  • The Dar es Salaam figures in section 01 apply national rates to the city’s 2022 census population and are indicative. Urban rates of raised blood pressure are often higher than the national average, so the true number may be larger.
  • No survey yet measures how much time and money Dar es Salaam households spend searching for health products. The evening shown in section 06 is illustrative. Afya Basics will measure it in pre-launch customer interviews and publish the result.
